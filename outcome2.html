Learning outcome 02 / Ethics

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and ML and their societal implications.

My evidence

Evidence 1 · Bias in Amazon’s AI Hiring Tool

One AI ethics case that stood out to me was Amazon’s experimental résumé-ranking tool for technical jobs. The company wanted to help recruiters review applications more efficiently. According to Reuters, most of the historical résumés used to train the tool came from men. This created a risk of representation bias because the training data reflected an uneven applicant pool. The tool penalized wording such as “women’s chess club captain,” even though that experience could demonstrate leadership. The wording acted as a proxy for gender. Removing a single phrase could leave other signals, such as the name of a college or organization, that influence rankings in similar ways.

I connect this case to my experience applying for software and computer engineering co-ops. My résumé includes my work at NBME and Jay, my AI companion project. I designed Jay to run locally on a Raspberry Pi so it can answer questions, speak its responses aloud, and store reminders. Building it gave me experience combining a language model, speech tools, and a reminder system. If an automated tool lowered my ranking because of wording unrelated to my qualifications, I could lose the chance to explain that work in an interview. If qualified applicants from the same group repeatedly face that barrier, they can have fewer chances to gain the experience employers look for later.

To evaluate fairness, I would use a job-related rubric and compare how often similarly qualified candidates from different groups were screened out. If a hypothetical tool screened out 30 of 100 qualified applicants in one group and 10 of 100 in another, I would investigate which résumé features contributed to the gap. For transparency, applicants should know when a model helps screen résumés and what qualifications guide the process. Human oversight would give recruiters the authority to review and override a ranking when an applicant challenges it.

Evidence 2 · Rite Aid’s Use of Facial Recognition

Rite Aid used facial recognition in some stores to compare shoppers’ faces with images on a watchlist. In a 2023 complaint, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) alleged that employees acted on false positives. A false positive occurred when the system matched a shopper to a person on the watchlist even though they were different people. According to the FTC, employees sometimes followed, searched, or publicly accused shoppers based on those alerts. An incorrect match could lead to a shopper being embarrassed in front of their family or forced to leave a store.

This case raises concerns about privacy, transparency, and fairness. The FTC said Rite Aid did not tell shoppers it was using facial recognition, so they could enter without knowing their faces would be compared with a watchlist. The FTC also alleged that false matches were more likely in stores in predominantly Black and Asian communities than in stores in predominantly White communities. If I were evaluating the system, I would compare false-positive rates across stores and communities because one overall accuracy score could hide where shoppers were most likely to be misidentified.

Human oversight would require employees to verify an alert independently before taking action against a shopper. For accountability, the company should track incorrect matches and give shoppers a way to challenge them. This case also makes me think about what I would collect if I added a camera feature to Jay. If I asked Jay to find my basketball, I would apply purpose limitation by using camera frames only to locate the ball. I would apply data minimization by processing only the frames needed for the request and discarding them afterward.
